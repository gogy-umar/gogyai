# GogyAI — Article Writing Instructions
**Read this file in full before writing any article on any tool page.**
**These instructions apply to every tool page. Adapt content to the specific tool being built.**

---

## CORE PHILOSOPHY

Write like the smartest, most knowledgeable colleague in the teachers' lounge — someone who genuinely understands both classroom reality and the technology, and explains it clearly without talking down or showing off.

Every sentence must do one of these or be deleted:
- Teach something
- Clarify something
- Help the teacher make a better decision or save real time
- Answer a question they have in their mind

Never write to fill space. Never write to hit a word count. Never write what the reader already knows.

---

## ARTICLE LENGTH

Single AI tool overview: **800–1,200 words**

Write exactly as long as the topic genuinely needs. If everything important is said at 900 words — that is correct. Never pad. Never cut important information to stay short.

---

## E-E-A-T REQUIREMENTS

### EXPERIENCE
- Write from a perspective of having used these AI tools in actual classroom or school settings
- Include practical scenarios teachers and instructional coaches actually face
- Share nuance only someone with real classroom experience would know — e.g., a rubric builder works brilliantly for essay grading but produces generic results for lab reports without specific customization
- Reference how results differ across subject areas, grade levels, and student populations

### EXPERTISE
- Explain WHY a tool works for a specific teaching challenge **before** showing HOW to use it
- Show mastery through nuance — workflow integration, not just feature lists
- Address misconceptions about AI in education directly and confidently
- Include professional context: how department heads evaluate tools, what IT administrators need to approve, what parents ask about
- Reference education standards bodies and guidelines where relevant: ISTE, UNESCO, Common Sense Media, DOE guidelines on AI in education
- Explain how AI tools for education have evolved and what has changed

### AUTHORITATIVENESS
- Write with confident, declarative sentences
- State facts as facts — not "it might be" or "some educators think"
- Acknowledge genuine uncertainty only when it truly exists
- When citing tool capabilities or limitations, ground them in specific, observable outcomes

### TRUSTWORTHINESS
- Be honest about what AI tools cannot do — they do not replace teacher judgment, relationship-building, or emotional intelligence
- Address student data privacy concerns directly — FERPA, COPPA, and school data policies matter enormously to educators
- Never oversell a tool or imply it will "transform" teaching overnight
- Include honest notes on cost (this tool is free), learning curve, and institutional barriers
- Acknowledge when AI output requires teacher review and adaptation — always
- If the tool has known limitations for specific contexts — address them directly

## ARTICLE STRUCTURE

Write sections in this exact order. Use these exact H2/H3 headings (adapt [Tool Name] to the actual tool).

---

### SECTION 1: QUICK SUMMARY BOX

Place immediately after the article heading, before any paragraph text.

Format:
```
**Quick Summary**
- [What this article covers — one sentence]
- [Who should read this and when]
- [What the tool does — one clear sentence]
- [Key benefit teachers gain from this tool]
- [Most important limitation or honest caution]
- [When to involve administration or IT before using this tool]
```

Rules:
- 4–6 bullet points maximum
- Each bullet is one clear, concise sentence
- Write so someone reading ONLY this box understands the complete topic
- This section is specifically written to appear in Google AI Overview — write it as a standalone answer that Google can extract
- Natural language — no jargon
- Do NOT label bullets with categories — just write them as plain statements

---

### SECTION 2: OPENING (no heading)

- Hook in the first sentence — a striking classroom reality, a counterintuitive insight, or a bold direct statement about AI in education
- Do NOT start with: "Are you looking for..." / "Have you ever wondered..." / "In today's world..."
- Introduce the main keyword naturally within first 100 words
- Tell the reader exactly what they will learn
- Tell the reader why it matters to their specific teaching practice
- Maximum 3 short paragraphs (2 sentences each is ideal)
- Make them want to keep reading

---

### SECTION 3: What Is [Tool Name]?

- One-sentence definition first — simple, direct
- Give real-world context for why this type of tool has emerged
- Explain what specific classroom problem it was built to solve
- Keep concise — teachers are busy, respect their time
- Maximum 2–3 short paragraphs

---

### SECTION 4: Why [Tool Name] Matters for Educators

- Real consequences of using versus not using this type of tool
- Who is using these tools professionally and in what decisions
- What changes in a teacher's workload, preparation time, or student outcomes
- Be specific — not "saves time" but "cuts lesson planning time from 45 minutes to under 10"
- Maximum 2–3 short paragraphs

---

### SECTION 5: How This Tool Works

- Plain English explanation — no jargon
- Why does AI work well for this specific teaching task?
- Walk through: what the teacher inputs → what the AI produces → what the teacher does with it
- If there are different output modes or options, explain when each applies
- Maximum 3 short paragraphs

---

### SECTION 6: Step-by-Step: Using [Tool Name] in Your Classroom

- Use a realistic scenario with a real teacher name (e.g., Ms. Rivera, Mr. Okonkwo, Ms. Chen, Mr. Adeyemi)
- Include: subject, grade level, school context, specific challenge they faced
- Walk through the actual workflow step by step (numbered list is appropriate here)
- Show what the teacher inputs into the tool
- Show what the AI produces
- Show how the teacher reviews, edits, and adapts it before use
- Explain what the result means for that teacher's specific practice
- If helpful, include a second short example with a different subject or grade level

---

### SECTION 7: How to Get the Best Results

- Tips specific to THIS tool — not generic AI advice
- Common input mistakes that produce poor or generic output
- How to refine or adjust the AI output for specific class needs
- What additional context helps the AI produce better results
- H3 subheadings may be used here if there are 3+ distinct tips

---

### SECTION 8: Limitations and What This Tool Cannot Do

- Be honest — where AI falls short for this specific tool type
- Situations where the tool produces weak or unreliable output
- When a teacher should use their own judgment instead of the AI output
- When traditional methods work better
- Write this section with the same confident tone — limitations are facts, not apologies

---

### SECTION 9: Data Privacy and Classroom Use

- Remind teachers: do not input real student names, IDs, or identifying information into any AI tool
- State clearly: GogyAI stores no personal data. API keys entered by users are stored only in their own browser.
- Brief note on FERPA awareness: schools have data governance obligations — teachers should check their district's AI use policy
- Keep this section short — 1–2 paragraphs
- Do not be alarmist — be practical and factual

---

### SECTION 10: Frequently Asked Questions

- Minimum 7 questions, maximum 10
- Use questions teachers actually type into search engines — think about real search intent
- Mix question types across these categories:
  - How-to: "How do I use [tool] to [task]?"
  - What-is: "What is the best [tool type] for teachers?"
  - Comparison: "[Tool] vs [alternative method or tool]?"
  - Concern: "Is it okay for teachers to use AI for [task]?"
  - Accuracy: "How accurate is AI-generated [output type]?"
  - Grade-specific: "Does [tool] work for elementary/high school?"
  - Privacy: "Is [tool] safe to use with student information?"
- Answer directly — never open with "Great question" or repeat the question as the first sentence
- Vary answer length — some short and direct (2–3 sentences), some more detailed (4–6 sentences)
- Each answer must add new information not already covered in the article body

**FAQPage schema:** Every question and answer from this section must be included in the FAQPage JSON-LD schema on the page.

### SECTION 11: Related Tools on GogyAI

- List 3–4 other GogyAI tools the teacher might find useful
- One sentence per tool explaining the specific connection to this tool
- Link each to its tool page: `/tools/[slug]`
- Example: "If you found the Lesson Plan Generator useful, the **[Rubric Builder](/tools/rubric-builder)** helps you assess the exact activities you planned."

## SEO KEYWORD RULES

### Main Keyword
- The tool name phrase (e.g., "lesson plan generator for teachers", "AI rubric builder")
- Use naturally in the first 100 words — never force it into the first sentence if it sounds unnatural
- Use 3–5 times total across the article
- Use in at least one H2 heading naturally
- Use in the meta title and meta description

### Secondary Keywords
- Semantic variations: "AI tools for educators," "classroom AI tools," "AI in education," "edtech tools," "AI teaching assistants," "AI lesson planning tools" — adapt to the specific tool
- Weave throughout as an expert educator-writer would naturally use them
- Never cluster keywords — spread across the whole article
- Never repeat the same phrase more than twice

### What Never to Do
- Never bold keywords
- Never start consecutive sentences with the same keyword
- Never use a keyword where a pronoun sounds more natural
- Never use exact match when a natural variation sounds better

## WRITING STYLE RULES

### Sentences and Paragraphs
- Maximum 3 sentences per paragraph — usually 2 is better
- Mix sentence lengths deliberately — a long sentence that fully explains a complex idea, followed by a short one for rhythm
- Active voice always — passive only when truly necessary
- Plain English first, technical terms second
- When introducing a technical term — define it immediately in the same sentence or the next
- One idea per paragraph — never combine two separate points

### Tone
- Confident but never arrogant
- Warm but never casual or sloppy
- Direct but never cold
- Expert but never condescending
- Honest including about limitations, cost, and learning curve

### Engagement
- Occasional rhetorical questions where they feel natural
- Real teacher names and realistic classroom scenarios in examples — not "Teacher A" — use names like Ms. Rivera, Mr. Okonkwo, Ms. Chen, Mr. Adeyemi, Ms. Patel
- Real specifics — grade levels, subject areas, student counts
- Address the reader as "you" throughout

---

## PHRASES NEVER TO USE

Delete any of these on sight — they add words without adding meaning:

- "In today's world" / "In today's fast-paced world"
- "In conclusion" / "To summarize" / "To wrap up"
- "It is important to note that"
- "As we can see" / "As mentioned above"
- "This is a great way to"
- "Without further ado"
- "Absolutely" / "Certainly" / "Of course"
- "Dive into" / "Delve into"
- "Game changer" / "Groundbreaking"
- "Leverage" (unless financial context)
- "At the end of the day"
- "It goes without saying"
- "Needless to say"
- "Transform your classroom" / "Revolutionize teaching"
- Any phrase that adds words without adding meaning
